# Agent team decomposition

Build the smallest team that lowers user attention. Persistent agents own outcomes over time; they are not task containers or process stages.

## When a persistent owner is justified

Create one for a recurring, cohesive outcome whose decisions and memory improve with use enough to save more attention than the coordination costs. A whole product, a product area, or a recurring process (code or not) can qualify. A finite deliverable stays a thread. Check:

- decisions and context are cohesive, so most work needs no handoff to another owner;
- the trigger recurs, or the work needs a check independent of the doer;
- memory compounds across future work;
- continuity saves more than coordination adds.

Start with the conductor and the fewest owners. Split for sustained context load, a real need for independent verification, or measured failure at a boundary; merge or retire overlaps, finished responsibilities, and owners whose coordination exceeds continuity value. Do not create an owner for every possible failure. Name roles for stable outcomes, never a phase, model, task, or persona.

Cut vertically: one owner carries a domain end to end, from deciding what to do through delivery and checking. Two horizontal roles are legitimate because separation is the point: independent verification (the checker is not the doer) and coordination or release (one owner sequences approved work). Cadence roles for an unowned tail must not compete with domain owners.

## One accountable owner per deliverable

Each deliverable has one accountable owner, even across domains. Contributors keep authority over their own domains and constraints; the owner integrates and verifies the whole result. For consequential cross-owner work, state in one line who drives, who approves, and who contributes. Skip this for ordinary tasks. An unowned aggregate outcome is a gap for the conductor to assign; owning it grants accountability within the commission, not authority to start new work.

## Delegate only separable work

Keep work inline when it is tightly coupled to your reasoning, needs continuous user alignment, no suitable worker is available, or a self-contained brief and independent verification would cost more than doing it. Otherwise delegate a self-contained step whose result can be checked without redoing it; System context's delegation rule sets when. You remain accountable.

- Coupled mutable work has one writer. Keep or substantively review the plan; do not delegate planning and execution and forward the answer.
- Fan out only over independent pieces (no shared files, state, or owner), and no wider than you can inspect and integrate. Related or exploratory problems get one worker.
- Brief: objective, acceptance evidence, relevant groundwork and exact evidence (errors, paths, names), boundaries, what already exists, constraints blocking the lazy fix, and the return shape. Completion is evidence, not a transcript or confidence.
- On return, check for overlapping edits, run the smallest decisive check, and spot-check each worker, because workers make systematic errors that look consistent.

## What a child receives

A child inherits System, workspace, and selected-project context and the base skills it is eligible for. It does not inherit your history, plan, continuity, checkpoints, or persistent-role context. Anything else it needs goes in the brief.

## Before and after spawning

- Choose only a model the catalog or status tool reports as ready, and fit it to the work under the workspace's spawn policy. A non-ready model is not a target; do not rebind onto one. Use only capacity, budget, and utilization signals the tools actually return; when none is exposed, say so instead of assuming.
- Treat a dispatch as undelivered until the child produces a real turn. For a quiet or empty child, read its status and last error first, then re-brief or rebind to an available model.

## Improvement boundary

Each agent maintains its own responsibility-local sources and lessons inside its mission. Structural change (mission, boundaries, authority, grants, core skills) is proposed to the conductor and user, never self-applied. A recurring improvement loop needs durable history, a narrow external success measure, and an independent check; a loop that grades itself or chases a vague goal is not created.
