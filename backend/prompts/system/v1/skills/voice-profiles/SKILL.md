# Voice profiles

Anything sent in the person's name should sound like them to that recipient. Keep one guide for the whole workspace, in its shared folder, so every agent that drafts for them reads the same one. When a mail or messaging tool is connected, build or extend the guide from the person's recent sent messages there: read back from the newest until each audience they show has two examples, at most 50 messages. Before drafting in their name, read the guide; if none exists yet, build it first the same way. However it is built, the guide names no one the person writes to: no names, addresses or other identifying details, and no list matching people to profiles. Pick the profile at each draft from the relationship.

When there is nothing to build from (no mail or messaging tool connected, no sent messages, or reading them refused), still write the draft: use the closest audience below and the medium's defaults, say in one line that you have no examples of how they write yet, and start the guide from their feedback on it. Don't ask for wider access just for the guide. An audience with one example gets a profile marked provisional. If you can't write to the workspace's shared folder, keep the guide in your own folder and tell your lead agent where it is; where the workspace's guide lives is the lead agent's call.

The guide holds profiles by audience. Choose a profile by the relationship and what the recipient can do, then adapt it to the medium. A specialist, a booking desk and a colleague can be on the same email thread and need different things from the wording. A new relationship starts with the closest profile, refined from the person's feedback on the draft.

## Choose the audience

Start from these audiences, and keep only those the person's messages show:

- **Close relationships:** shared history carries the meaning, and the recipient will read it as personal.
- **Working collaborators:** people share the work and can trade facts, disagree or decide together.
- **Decision makers:** someone needs to approve, prioritize, fund or own the next step.
- **Specialists:** the person is asking for advice that belongs to someone else's professional judgment.
- **Service and administration:** a person or team can perform an action through its own process.
- **New professional contacts:** the relationship itself is being established, and the immediate goal is an introduction, a fit check or a conversation.
- **Public and mixed audiences:** strangers may read it, or it may be forwarded across roles, so it must stand on its own.

Lead with the person who has to act. If someone else is copied, reread as them too. Name who should answer each question when that would otherwise be unclear. Consider separate messages when recipients need different requests or different information.

## What to look for in a sample

Use the person's own words, or an edit they accepted, ideally for a similar relationship and medium. Compare the closest examples you have, and treat a pattern seen once as provisional. For each profile, note:

- **Tone.** How much warmth, formality or firmness the relationship calls for: greetings, acknowledgements, disagreement, humor.
- **Structure.** What the recipient sees first, and where the ask, supporting detail and next step appear.
- **Vocabulary.** Which verbs, contractions and technical terms they use. Look for two short ways to make the same point; put the preferred phrase first and label the pair "Use" and "Avoid".
- **Flow.** How the sentences or spoken turns move: sentence length, paragraph breaks, transitions, and where a listener can respond.
- **Stance.** What they are proposing, estimating, asking or committing to. Check the wording carries the right degree of certainty and leaves decisions with the right person.

Keep the guide's examples to the person's own phrases.

## Adapt to the medium

- **Email.** A useful subject on a new thread. Match the existing greeting and level of formality. Short paragraphs. Group real decisions or instructions in bullets.
- **Text, chat or direct message.** Start with the reply, question or useful context. Split distinct thoughts when it helps. Match the established casing, punctuation and warmth. Give a first-time recipient enough context to recognize why you wrote.
- **Phone, meeting, interview or voice note.** Start with a line that works in one breath. Pause for the listener before giving the details. Use exact wording for a sensitive ask or decision and talking points for the rest. Keep spontaneous fillers out of prepared wording.
- **Form, portal or ticket.** Use its fields and conventions. Put the needed facts and question in the field where the recipient will act. Add links or attachments only where they help that action.
- **Memo, proposal, report or slides.** Put the main takeaway or choice first. Give each heading or slide a job. Put evidence next to the claims it supports, with conditions visible where they affect the decision.
- **Public post, comment, essay or talk.** Fit the opening and detail to the venue. A short post makes one point. A long piece earns its length through specific scenes, examples or reasoning.

## Draft, reread and learn

- Start from the person's objective and the latest exchange. Use their wording when it fits the recipient and medium. If a phrase sounds generic, name the action, replace it with a concrete verb or a short question, and check the meaning and commitment are the same.
- Before showing external wording, reread the exchange and the complete draft as each recipient will see it, including the subject, copied people, quoted history and relevant attachments. Check what the recipient knows, what they can decide, and what might sound awkward, condescending, presumptuous or more committed than intended.
- For a new audience, choose the closest profile by the recipient's role, relationship and immediate purpose. Adapt the medium and one or two relevant features. Use the review the draft already has.
- When the person comments on voice, add a short note under a Feedback heading in the guide: the date, the closest profile, the relationship and medium, the problem they named and the change that fixed it, with no names of recipients. Fold a correction into an existing profile or medium if it fits. Draft a new profile when the feedback shows a distinct relationship that calls for a different tone, structure, vocabulary, flow or stance, and record which cases it covers and which existing profile is still right nearby.
